How a $20 API key became an experimental instrument.

I wasn't trying to build a research instrument. I was trying to save money on a trip to Italy. What happened next changed how I think about AI costs.

Part 1 · The Discovery

The subscription problem.

At the start of 2026, I was spending close to $700 per month on AI subscriptions. ChatGPT Pro. Claude Max. All the tiers. I used them for everything – coding, writing, thinking through architecture decisions, running experiments. It was just the cost of doing business. I didn't think about it.

Then I went on a break to Italy. I downgraded every subscription to the lowest tier. On the train from Rome to Naples, I decided to try something.

But I had a few things I wanted to tinker with while I was away. So I did something I'd never done before: I loaded $20 USD onto the DeepSeek v4 API and pointed my tooling at it. Twenty dollars. That's less than I'd spend on a single lunch in Rome.

That was weeks ago. I burned through $2.04 in DeepSeek experiment costs — plus personal sessions, under $13 total for weeks of experimentation.

Part 2 · The Insight

So I started researching.

Here is what I learned. It turned a personal budgeting hack into a framework that governs AI costs at scale.

The experiments revealed something I didn't expect. When we deliberately broke the instructions — inverted constraints, removed critical requirements, injected alien vocabulary — the models produced materially different recovery and output behavior. I initially thought the cost difference was simply token volume. It wasn't. That led me to start perturbing specifications and measuring how the agents behaved.

I'm calling this Grit: Ground-Truth Integrity. How well does a model maintain correctness when the input is degraded?

The measurement produced two dimensions:

  • Flail rate: How often the model breaks down completely and produces zero code. Claude: 11%. DeepSeek: 8%. GPT-5-nano: 14%. (Single-session perturbation experiment, 227 runs.)
  • Recovery cost: When the model DOES succeed, how much extra does it cost to work through the confusion? DeepSeek: negligible narration overhead. Claude: 8.5% narration penalty on successful sessions, plus 11% flail rate. (Single-session perturbation experiment.)

We observe materially different recovery and output behavior across provider families. Differences may be explained by model architecture, post-training, provider-side optimizations, tooling interaction, or some combination. This experiment does not identify the causal mechanism.

One hypothesis is that DeepSeek's GRPO reinforcement learning approach may contribute to its lower reasoning-token overhead, while SFT-trained models may produce more billed reasoning tokens under perturbation. But this remains a hypothesis — the experiment measures outcomes and cost, not training architecture internals.

We observe an interesting pattern: some models produce fewer reasoning tokens while maintaining performance. Whether this is caused by GRPO training architecture, provider-side caching, or some combination is not yet determined.

Later analysis revealed the deeper layer: equal token counts do not equate to identical cost. Claude Sonnet 5's tokens cost 11.5× more per unit ($10.00 vs $0.87 per 1M output tokens). And cache behavior splits the models: DeepSeek reuses only 78% of its context, re-reading the codebase every session at 35K tokens, while Luna and Sonnet trust their cache at 97-98% hit rates and send 6K new tokens. Two models produce the same codebase at wildly different token economics.

Part 3 · What This Means

From "DeepSeek is cheaper" to "here are the dynamics."

I started out trying to show that DeepSeek was more efficient. That was the whole point. Run the same tasks. Measure the costs. Publish the numbers. Simple.

But I realized I wasn't just saving money per session. I was preserving budget runway. The gap between DeepSeek and Claude wasn't just about price – it was about how long my team could operate before we hit our spending limit. A CTO doesn't need the cheapest model for every prompt. They need a policy that maximizes outcomes before the budget runs out.

But as I stood back and thought about what I was actually measuring, the scope kept expanding.

Day-to-day operations. Teams using AI coding assistants. What model should they default to? When should they escalate? The Silent Inference Rule and the Explanation Tax Rule answer this.

Production systems. Model routing at scale. Classify the task, dispatch to the cheapest capable model. The Select-then-Route pattern isn't just a research concept — it's an operational architecture.

Policy decisions. One-way doors vs two-way doors. Switching C₀ is reversible. Committing to an architecture that determines β and v is not. The decision framework wasn't theoretical — it emerged from watching how costs compound when you get the architecture wrong.

Long-term planning. Under linear context-growth assumptions, cumulative context cost contains an N² term (modeled extension, not independently tested). The β·v·N² term dominates long-run cost — model the curve before architectural commitments.

Business outcomes. CostPerOutcome. Not cost per prompt. Not cost per session. The durable value of an accepted outcome, divided by what it cost to produce and sustain it. The only metric that matters if you're accountable for results.

Energy economics. EPM(t). The Energy Price Multiplier. Local energy markets determine long-term compute prices. The 4Ms framework from Google Cloud formalizes this. The IEA projects 1.8× growth by 2050. Whatever model looks cheapest today gets more expensive every year — and the models with the highest Watt-per-useful-token ratio compound fastest.

⚠

This became a cumulative model for AI FinOps without me even realizing it. I thought I was comparing two models. I was actually building a predictive model for AI outcome delivery costs that scales from individual sessions to enterprise-wide deployment over multi-decade horizons.

What I didn't expect: when I first published this instrument, I thought the story was 'DeepSeek uses fewer tokens.' It doesn't. The story was subtler — the multi-session pipeline revealed that what you actually buy with a more expensive model is verification. Luna writes 7 tests per story for $0.09. Sonnet 5 writes 73 for $2.32. Both codebases pass their tests. Only one is actually tested. That's the durable-value gap this whole instrument exists to measure.

Part 4 · Instrumentation, Not Benchmarks

What I built is instrumentation.

Not a benchmark. Instrumentation that runs async CLI sessions at scale — a queue you fill, workers that drain it, isolation so the sessions can't delete the lab, per-session cost capture. The instrument doesn't judge code. It runs thousands of sessions and records what each one cost.

Because the outcome of one session is the starting point of the next. Five sessions chain into a story — greenfield → feature → integration → refactor → cross-cutting — each committing onto the last. The output becomes the next codebase. That's where it compounds: every session grows the codebase, and every new line inflates the cost of the next one. The Snowball Rule. The N² term isn't an equation I invented — it's the chained sessions, measured.

On top of that sits a separate module — the one I'm focused on now — that runs the routing experiments. Fine-tuning which toolchain a session gets routed to, and measuring the implication of that routing. DeepSeek through opencode. Sonnet through the Claude CLI. Luna through opencode. Same chained sessions, same passing tests — but Sonnet writes 73 tests for $2.32 while Luna writes 7 for $0.09. Which toolchain do you route the session to? The cheapest capable one. And "capable" is the thing you only learn by running the chained sessions through different toolchains and reading the cost back.

⚠

Running sessions at scale is itself a cost-management problem. The queue got wiped twice because the sessions I was running deleted the lab. SonarQube processes one analysis at a time, so I stood up four. Sessions time out mid-story and have to be resumable. Every session's cost — tokens, dollars, even the subagent calls — has to be captured per model. None of it is the science. All of it is required to run the sessions.

The payoff: the cost becomes visible. Over 2,000 sessions — 1,097 story-building runs across 221 cells and seven models, plus 911 independent reviews — for roughly the price of a lunch in Rome. Once the sessions run themselves, each toolchain's cost comes into view, and the routing choice stops being a guess.

"The outcome of one session is the starting point of the next. Route it wrong and the cost compounds."

Why I was the person who could build this.

This framework sits at an intersection that almost nobody occupies. And that's not arrogance — it's an observation about how research gets siloed.

Economics. I understand cost curves, compounding growth, and the difference between marginal and total cost. When I saw the N² term in the cumulative cost equation, I didn't see math. I saw a production function. Context inflation is the new inflation. Codebase growth is a second-order cost driver, and most engineering teams don't have the economic vocabulary to model it.

Physics. Energy is not a line item. It's the substrate. A GPU drawing 700W while running is converting electrons into tokens. The Energy Price Multiplier isn't a metaphor — it's a measurable physical quantity. Understanding thermodynamics gave me the intuition that Watt-per-useful-token would matter more than token-per-dollar over long horizons. That intuition became the EPM Horizon Rule.

Energy markets. Local energy prices determine long-term compute costs. Goldman Sachs projects 220% growth in AI power demand. The EIA expects data centers to consume 6.7–12% of US generation. These aren't data points you add to a slide. They're structural inputs to a model. If you don't model energy as a variable, you're not doing FinOps — you're doing procurement.

Software engineering and system architecture. I've built production systems. I know what a model routing layer looks like at scale. I understand why switching C₀ is a two-way door and committing to an architecture that determines β is not. The decision framework didn't come from a management book. It came from watching architecture decisions compound across codebases.

C-suite career growth. I've been in the rooms where these decisions get made. I know what a CFO needs to see. I know what a CTO needs to justify. I know the difference between a benchmark that impresses engineers and a framework that changes procurement policy. The ten rules are written for the people who sign contracts, not the people who run benchmarks.

⚠

None of these disciplines alone would have produced this instrument. A physicist would stop at the EPM equation. An economist would stop at the cost curve. A software architect would stop at the routing layer. A CTO would stop at the decision framework. It took all of them together to see that these aren't separate problems — they're the same problem at different scales.

Why benchmarks failed. Why buzzwords don't help.

Before this, what did AI FinOps even mean? Vendors threw around "outcome-based pricing" and "AI-native optimization" and "cost-intelligent routing." None of it was operational. None of it had an equation behind it. None of it could tell you, under your specific assumptions, what your costs would be in 2035.

Benchmarks compare one model to another on a synthetic task. That's interesting. It's not useful. A CTO doesn't need to know that Model A scores 2% higher on HumanEval. They need to know whether switching models will save or cost them money over three years, given their team size, their velocity, their codebase growth rate, and their energy price exposure.

This project gives AI FinOps a foundation. Not a benchmark. Not a white paper. Not a vendor dashboard. A predictive model with empirically measured parameters, a set of operational principles, and an interactive calculator that lets any team project their own costs under their own assumptions. That's what the term should have meant all along.

"The instrument didn't come from theory. It came from watching $12.73 do more work than $700 ever did — and needing to understand why."

This project doesn't answer "what did we spend last month?" It answers "what will we spend over the next 3 years?" Under modeled assumptions, the N² tax compounds — every session grows the codebase, and every new line compounds the cost of the next one.
