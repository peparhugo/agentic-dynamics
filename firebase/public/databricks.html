In July 2026, Databricks published their playbook for reducing AI coding costs at scale — based on surveys of Stripe, Coinbase, Uber, and Ramp. The sources reached related operational conclusions through different evidence: Databricks contributes enterprise survey evidence, while this instrument contributes 344 controlled perturbation game reports across 205 worktrees, 7 active models, and 3 architectures. The Efficiency Frontier and Grit

Databricks defines the Efficiency Frontier as "the set of models that have the best price point for a given level of intelligence." We define Grit as Ground-Truth Integrity — how well a model maintains correctness when given degraded, contradictory, or incomplete instructions.

Complementary concepts examined through different methods. The Efficiency Frontier concerns price for a level of intelligence; Grit concerns integrity under degraded input.

Databricks

Their evidence

Surveys of Stripe, Coinbase, Uber, and Ramp. Directional estimates. "Public benchmarks do a poor job of indicating real-world performance on coding tasks."

Our evidence

Lever 1: Shift to Efficient Models

Databricks

Their finding

"Rapidly adopting newer, more efficient models delivers the largest cost wins." ~50% savings. "The efficiency frontier is advancing far faster than the intelligence frontier."

This instrument

Our measurement

$0.07/story (DeepSeek Flash) vs $1.54/story (Claude Haiku). 23× measured cost gap on average cost per story; run head-to-head at matched 100% correctness, DeepSeek Flash and GPT-5.6 Sol still differ by ~33×. The gap is pricing architecture, not capability. Correctness across the 3-way operator classification shows different model rankings by class — no single provider dominates every class.

Where they complement each other: Their survey focused on price/intelligence tradeoffs from enterprise deployment data. Our controlled experiments measure integrity under stress — revealing that a model can be cheap and smart on a benchmark yet collapse under inverted constraints. Grit captures something the Efficiency Frontier wasn't designed to measure.

Lever 2: Dynamic Request and Task Routing

Databricks

Their finding

"Internal results suggest that our AI Gateway Smart Router is able to consistently reduce average task cost by more than 30%." Request-level, task-level, and escalation routing.

This instrument

Our measurement

Grit-routed strategy: 100% correctness at $0.0099/session average (n=11), routed 9-of-11 to DeepSeek Flash and 2-of-11 to DeepSeek Pro. Per-task routing recommendations across 9 analyzed task types: 7/9 default to DeepSeek Flash, 2/9 to DeepSeek Pro, and 6/9 have an escalation path to a premium model on Grit failure. DeepSeek-only beats every premium-only strategy on both cost and correctness in this corpus.

Where they complement each other: Their routing strategy is based on task complexity taxonomy from real-world deployment data. Our Grit-gated routing adds stress-resilience to the equation — measuring flail rate under perturbation to ensure cheaper models don't collapse when instructions degrade.

Lever 3: Visibility, Tripwires, and Progressive Budgets

Databricks

Their finding

"Instead of a hard user spending cap, most companies are adopting a more nuanced and progressive approach." Visibility, spend gates, downshifting, suspension. ~10% savings.

This instrument

Our measurement

WOC = 1.00 — measured first-pass success rate across the current 205-worktree, 344-report corpus, with 0% measured retry rate so far. Rule 7: Budget Ceiling (Tmax = Budget / Cjob). Rule 8: Cascade Rule (escalate only on Grit failure). Their "downshifting" = our cascade. Their "spend gates" = our WOC alerts. The escalation ladder itself is measured: DeepSeek→GPT-5.6 Sol costs 58.5× more per job, DeepSeek→premium 70.1× more, and a human fallback ($5/job) 73.4× more.

Where they complement each other: Their approach handles reactive budgeting — detecting and responding to overspend in real-time from live deployment data. This instrument adds forecasting: the Snowball Rule (N²) and EPM Horizon model multi-year cost trajectories, projecting costs before they compound.

Lever 4: Reducing Token Overhead

Databricks

Their finding

"Simple tuning of our harness and caching settings led to an almost 50% reduction in the number of generated tokens." Compaction, less chatty harnesses, cache tuning. ~10% + 50% token reduction.

This instrument

Our measurement

Output decomposition: Claude flails on 11% of sessions (zero code, narration only) vs DeepSeek 8%. When models succeed, DeepSeek carries negligible narration overhead (0.0% penalty), Claude carries 8.5%. DeepSeek is the only provider in this corpus that bills reasoning tokens on a separate, cheaper channel — other providers bill reasoning as ordinary output tokens, so narration and code cost the same per token. Cache asymmetry: premium cache-write pricing vs cheap cache-read pricing produces a similar-shaped effect — one provider pays a premium to write context to cache every session, the other reads a much larger cache at a fraction of the rate. Same underlying insight as their token-overhead lever, arrived at through calibrated measurement rather than harness tuning.

Where they complement each other: Their surveys and experiments identified that token overhead exists. Our stress tests measured why it happens — the model's architectural default to conversational mode under perturbation — making it actionable at the model selection level as well as the tool tuning level. If you're tuning your tools to reduce verbosity, you're working against the model's training distribution. Routing to a model with lower narrative overhead may be more cost-effective.

Where Each Adds Unique Insight

Databricks brings industry scale — real-world deployment data from companies processing millions of AI workflows daily, validated against enterprise budgets. Their surveys capture operational patterns at a breadth that controlled experiments alone cannot match: adoption lifecycles, organizational friction, and the full cost of change management.

The Agentic Dynamics instrument contributes methodology that enterprise surveys can't provide: controlled reproducibility, perturbation stress testing that reveals model fragility invisible on clean benchmarks, provenance-tagged measurements at the session level, and an open-source instrument deployable against any workload.

Perturbation stress testing

Measures Grit through 10 calibrated degradation operators — revealing that a model performing well on benchmarks may fail entirely under degraded instructions.

Model behavior under degraded input

3-way perturbation classification — specification corruption, objective mutation, process perturbation. Correctness and cost by class, not a single aggregate score; see the Evidence page for the current breakdown by model.

Compounding and forecasting

Snowball Rule (N²): codebase growth compounds quadratically. EPM Horizon: energy prices inflate compute costs. Survival Horizon: how many sessions before bankruptcy?

Attractor basin topology

The shape of a model's failure space. Wide-shallow basins explore efficiently. Narrow-deep basins recover expensively. Collapsed basins flail. Measured, not theorized.

Autonomous workforce model

Batch economics (50% discount, 72-hour horizon). Escalation cascades. SLA buffers. Workforce capacity modeling — projecting autonomous agent workloads beyond individual developer use.

Reproducible evidence

Provenance-tagged measurements [M]/[C]/[H]/[X], open-source under Apache 2.0, inspectable down to individual session transcripts. Every measurement is independently verifiable.

Where they converge: Databricks and this instrument examine four related operational areas with different scopes. Their surveys captured operational patterns at enterprise scale — from companies processing millions of AI coding workflows daily. Our 344 controlled game reports and 772 story sessions provide calibrated measurements under controlled stress. Together they offer complementary evidence: enterprise survey findings and provenance-tagged, reproducible instrument results that anyone can test against their own workloads. None of the comparisons above are product claims — they're operational hypotheses grounded in this corpus, meant to be tested against yours.
